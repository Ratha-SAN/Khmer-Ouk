ប្រវត្តិអុកចត្រង្គHistory and origins of Ouk Chatrang

Ouk Chatrang is the traditional form of chess in Cambodia. This page is a short overview for players, not a scholarly history. Where the sources disagree, or where we are not certain, we say so rather than guess.

គ្រួសារអុកA member of the chaturanga family

The word chatrang in the name comes from the Sanskrit chaturanga, which means "four limbs" or "four divisions", usually taken to refer to the four parts of an ancient army: infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariots. Chaturanga is generally regarded as the ancestor of the chess family. The usual account places it in India, around the sixth century CE, and follows its spread westward through Persia, where it became shatranj, then through the Islamic world and into Europe, where it eventually developed into modern international chess.

The game also travelled eastward. In Southeast Asia it developed into several closely related games, including Thai Makruk, Burmese Sittuyin, Lao chess and Cambodia's Ouk Chatrang. Makruk and Ouk Chatrang in particular share a recognisable design: pieces that move a short way, pawns that begin on the third rank, and no castling; the other relatives differ in their details. Chinese xiangqi and Japanese shogi are often grouped in the wider family as well.

Exactly when and by what route the game reached Cambodia is not something we can state with confidence, and we would rather say that than invent a date.

ឈ្មោះនៃកូនអុកThe names of the pieces

The Khmer names say a lot about how the game was imagined. Several are ordinary words of everyday Khmer.

Compare this with the international set. Where chess has a castle, Cambodian chess has a boat. Where chess has foot soldiers, Cambodian chess has fish. The Neang and the Koul also move far less than a modern queen or bishop, which is the biggest single difference in feel between the two games.

ច្បាប់What makes the rules distinctive

  • Short-range pieces. The Neang moves one square diagonally; the Koul moves one square diagonally or one square straight forward. Only the Tuuk and the Ses reach across the board.
  • Trey on the third rank. The armies start close together, and a Trey promotes (to a Neang) when it reaches the sixth rank.
  • First-move jumps. The Sdach may jump like a knight on its first move, and the Neang may leap two squares straight forward on its first move. Sources disagree about exactly when these rights are lost, and the game offers more than one setting for this.
  • The counting rules. A weaker side may start a count, and if the stronger side cannot deliver checkmate before the limit, the game is drawn. The limits quoted in different sources vary slightly. See the counting rules page.
  • No castling.

ផ្សេងពីអុកអន្តរជាតិOuk Chatrang and its neighbours

Because Thai Makruk is so close, players often assume the two games are identical. They share most of their rules but differ in details: the names of the pieces, how the opening jumps are treated and the exact counting limits. The game on this site follows the Cambodian names and the Cambodian rules as described in the sources available to us, and where those sources differ it offers settings, not one fixed answer.
