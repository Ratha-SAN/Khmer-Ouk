Most pieces in Ouk Chatrang move only a short way, so the game rewards patience, structure and careful counting more than long-range attacks. The advice below follows from the rules of the game and from the way the game's own computer opponent weighs a position. Every diagram was checked by the game engine, so each answer is a real, legal move.

តម្លៃនៃកូនអុកនីមួយៗWhat each piece is worth

Before you trade anything, know what you are giving up. The table shows the values the game's computer opponent uses to score positions, next to the same numbers rescaled so that a Trey is 1.

These numbers are a rule of thumb. They are a modelling choice of this game, not an official table, and the Sdach is deliberately left out because losing it ends the game. What matters is the order: Tuuk, then Ses, then Koul, then Neang, then Trey. A few useful consequences:

  • A Tuuk (about 5.8) is worth more than a Ses and a Trey together (about 5.2), so giving a Tuuk for a Ses and a Trey is a loss.
  • Two Koul (about 5.6) are close to one Tuuk; a Koul and a Neang (about 4.6) are close to a Ses.
  • A Neang is worth less than two Trey, so a Neang is not worth sacrificing two Trey for.

ការបើកOpening principles

Contact comes early. The Trey start on the third rank, so only two empty ranks separate the armies (international chess leaves four). Pieces are in range of each other after a couple of moves, so do not spend the first moves on slow, idle manoeuvres.

Advance the Trey in connected chains. A Trey captures one square diagonally forward, which means it also protects the two squares diagonally in front of it. A Trey on d4 guards c5 and e5. A Trey that steps to e5 with a friend on d4 or f4 behind it can be captured only at a price. Two or three Trey moving together cover each other; a single Trey wandering forward is usually lost.

Bring out the Ses early. The Ses is the only piece that jumps on every move, which is a big advantage on a crowded board where every other piece is blocked by its own side. A Ses near the centre reaches up to eight squares.

Use the first-move jumps while they last. The Neang may leap two squares straight forward on its first move, and the Sdach may jump like a knight on its first move. Each right is gone once that piece has moved, so the opening is the time to use them: the Neang leap develops a piece in one move, and the Sdach jump can tuck the king away from an open file. Remember there is no castling to do that job. (Two optional rules in the game's settings remove the jumps earlier, after the first capture or once an enemy Tuuk lines up with the Sdach, so on those settings the opening matters even more.)

ទូកPut the Tuuk to work

The Tuuk is by far your strongest piece. Keep it behind your Trey while the position is closed, and move it to an open rank or file as soon as one appears. A Tuuk on the seventh rank, as in the diagram on the checkmate patterns page, can pin the enemy Sdach to its back rank. Two Tuuk working together can drive an unsupported Sdach to the edge of the board. Do not trade a Tuuk for anything less than another Tuuk unless you are winning something larger.

ការពារស្ដេចKeep your Sdach guarded

The Neang and the Koul are the cheapest pieces apart from the Trey, but they are the best defenders. They move one square at a time, so they belong next to the Sdach, where they cover its escape squares and can capture an attacker that comes close. Be careful not to crowd the Sdach with your own pieces: the smothered mate on the checkmate page shows how a Sdach hemmed in by its own Tuuk and Trey can be mated by a single Ses.

គោលThink before you advance the Koul

The Koul moves one square diagonally in any direction, or one square straight forward, but never straight back. It can retreat only diagonally, so before you push it forward ask which squares it will be able to return to. The same rule shapes defence: a Koul cannot protect the piece standing directly behind it. That blind spot decides the last tactic below.

ល្បិចTactics to look for

Before every move ask three questions. What does my move attack? What does it leave undefended? Can my opponent capture something for free? The four positions below are real puzzles from the game. In each one it is White's move, the green arrow is the answer, and the diagram is checked by the engine.

1. Take what is undefended

A Black Tuuk stands in front of White's Tuuk with nothing protecting it. White simply captures it, and the capture gives check as well. Always scan for pieces that nothing defends, on both sides.

2. Let a Trey do the capturing

A Trey captures diagonally forward, not straight ahead. Here a Black Tuuk has wandered onto the diagonal in front of White's Trey, and the cheapest piece on the board wins the most valuable one.

3. The Ses fork

A fork is one move that attacks two things at once. The Ses jump from b5 to d6 gives check to the Sdach on e8 and attacks the Tuuk on c8. Black must save the Sdach, and White then takes the Tuuk. Forks are the Ses's speciality, because its attack comes from a square no short-range piece can guard in advance.

4. The Koul's blind spot

The Black Koul on d6 looks as if it protects the Tuuk on d7 right behind it. It does not, because a Koul can never capture straight backward. White's Koul takes the Tuuk for nothing.

ការដូរកូនអុកTrading and the counting rules

When you are ahead, trading pieces usually helps, because an endgame is where you can force checkmate. But Ouk Chatrang has a clock on the endgame: the counting rules let the weaker side start a count and claim a draw if checkmate does not arrive in time. The limits are short, only 8 with two Tuuk and 16 with one, so a material lead is not enough. You also need to know how to mate quickly. The counting rules page has the full table.

When you are behind, the same rules give you a plan: trade down. Once you have three pieces or fewer, and fewer than your opponent, you may start counting from 1 with a limit of 64 (Board's Honor). With only a bare Sdach left and no unpromoted Trey on the board, you may start the Piece's Honor count instead.

ការឡើងនាងPromotion is a gain, not a win

A Trey that reaches the sixth rank becomes a Neang at once. In the table above that is a gain of about 0.8 of a Trey, which is real but modest, because a Neang is the weakest of the major pieces. Promotion is still valuable in two ways. A Neang next to your Sdach is a superb mating helper (see the quiet Neang check on the checkmate page). And a Trey that promotes is one fewer unpromoted Trey on the board, which is one of the conditions for the Piece's Honor count. Do not assume, as in international chess, that a promoted piece decides the game by itself.

ល្បែងចុងBasic endgames

Two endgames are worth learning by heart. With a Sdach and a Tuuk against a bare Sdach, you must bring your own Sdach close and use it to cover squares while the Tuuk delivers the final check; this is the box mate. With two Tuuk, you build a ladder: one Tuuk shuts off a rank, the second shuts off the next, and the enemy Sdach is pushed to the edge. Both are on the checkmate patterns page. Practise them against the computer, and remember the count.

ទម្លាប់ល្អHabits that make you stronger

  • Check what your opponent's last move attacks before you start your own plan.
  • Count the defenders of every piece you plan to capture, and the attackers of every piece you plan to leave.
  • After a game, use the Review button to step back through it and find the move where the game turned.
  • Solve the three daily puzzles. They are short checkmate and tactics problems and train exactly the patterns above.
  • Move up through the computer levels one at a time. After a win, the game offers a Next level button.
